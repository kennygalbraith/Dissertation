What is *curiosity*? Given the resurgence in *curiosity* from diverse approaches in the cognitive sciences, it's both difficult and controversial to define. So, I'll first present what I take to be rough definition of curiosity that helps reader delimit the scope of the phenomena without prejudging or assuming much about the phenomena. After, I present a my more opinionated take on what *curiosity* that  is operating in the background of [[Great (Ape) Questions]] and the dissertation more broadly.

## A Working Definition

Roughly, **Curiosity** is a
- Basic Affective State
- that *intrinsically* motivates
- behaviors that result in the reduction of uncertainty or ignorance --- that is, learning behaviors.

By "basic", I mean that curiosity is widely distributed in the the animal kingdom (Carruthers 2018, Panksepp 2011, Izard 2007), where homologous neural mechanisms can be found in simple invertebrates like nematodes (Calhoun et al. 2014).

Moreover, the idea that curiosity is *intrinsically* motivating requires more clarification. Roughly, this corresponds to the thought that learning behaviors are *not proximately motivated* by further extrinsic/instrumental aims. For instance, from the personal-level psychology of an individual whose curious about an topic X, their behaviors oriented towards acquiring information about X are guided by reward/value about learning *in itself*. They aren't *proximately motivated* to acquire information for other extrinsic aims, like *X will  make me a lot of money*. In the context of studying curiosity in non-human animals, this often amounts to the introduction of an experimental condition where agents will forego some instrumental cost (usually, sacrificing food) to acquire/learn some information.

However, it is important to note that this is not to say that there is no extrinsic value to learning about X  benefiting the learner. After all, in order for learning to be adaptive the value of acquiring information must bottom out in some instrumental good (increases to fitness). Rather, the point is that from the level of *proximate motivations*, acquiring information about X is intrinsically valuable. 

Last, the learning behaviors are ones that reduce uncertain or ignorant states of an organism. These states are ultimately what trigger curious mechanisms. For instance, a dog hear an unrecognizable sound in the bushes placing it in a state of ignorance. Curious mechanisms, then,  respond to ignorance by a suite of evaluative mechanisms (with content similar to *whether learning about what caused the sound is safe* and *what behaviors should one take* ) determining whether and/or what behaviors to employ. These behaviors could either be learned or innate: such as, a dog curiously sniffing in the direction of an unknown object, or following the familiar yet uncertain human-voice in the distance.

## An Opinionated View

Following [[The Naive View]],  I take it that *curiosity*  falls under the natural kind, *inquisitive*. As such, curiosity is partly defined by the fact that the relevant mental states/mechanisms serve a representational function --- learning/inquiry --- and so have certain semantic content in virtue of serving this function. Put more precisely, curiosity is partly defined by the fact that its contents are question-like in representation ---- call this, The Questioning Account of Curiosity (Friedman, 2013, Whitcomb 2010, Carruthers 2018, 2021).

The Questioning Account of Curiosity's core claim is that contents of curiosity are questions, e.g., *where is X*, *who is X*, *what is X*... This view of the contents of curiosity has been most developed in light of the empirical literature by Carruthers (2021). He claims that the contents of curiosity in organisms without meta-cognition can be gleaned from neuro-decision-making models. These models are often employed to explain behavior in perceptual discrimination or categorization tasks. For instance, judgements or behaviors that discrimnate whether two lines are similiar enough in angles, or whether a certain utterance, *smish*, sounds sufficiently like a word/non-word.

![[DDM.png]]
At the core, these models represent alternative categories, e.g.,, word/non-word or simliar/dissimlair in angle, by activity in certain neural populations. The abstract idea is that sensory information acculmutes evidence for alternatives representations in a noisy manner. As evidence accumulates the activity in the neural populations increases as they compete to hit a threshold. These threshold are determined by both a speed and realability trade-off,  where, say, lower thresholds indicate representations that are quick but necessarily the most realiable.

Ultimately, Carruthers claims that such models can also characterize the contents of curiosity. After all, the models share sufficiently similar semantic structure to our linguistic theorizing about interrogatives: they represent uncertainty through decisions about alternative representations of the world. In the case of curiousity, the idea is that there is category that represents *ignorance*. So, as animal meet an unknown object in their enviroment a variety of competing alternative represntations might come into play, *squirrel*, *wind*, *food*, but competing among them is an *ignorance representation*. In this way, these category wins-out when all other categories don't meet thresholds in time, and when it winsout, will be associated with various learning behaviors, like approaching the object.
