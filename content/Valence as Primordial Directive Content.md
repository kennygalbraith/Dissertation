**Abstract**:
The concept of *valence* is central to the affective sciences. It is often theorized as a basic component from which emotional states and other affective mechanisms are constituted. However, the sub-fields that make up the affective sciences frequently employ importantly differing notions of valence. This paper focuses on the role that valence plays in neuroscientific explanations. Unlike psychological approaches, valence in this distinct sense is not a feature of emotional experience, but rather a feature of neural mechanisms and their processing.

In this paper, I argue that valence, as conceived in neuroscience, functions as a primordial directive: a content-general representation of *good* and *bad*, whose content is derivable from its role in producing appetitive and aversive behavior. Drawing on our recent understanding of the neuro-anatomy from of fruit flies ([[Drosophilia, a case study]]), I show that the role valence plays in action and learning lends to the it being a genuinely directive form of representation. Finally, I consider broader philosophical conclusions about whether valence should be understood as primitive in two distinct senses: (i) as evolutionarily early-emerging, and (ii) as conceptually primitive, in that it does not depend on antecedent evaluative notions.

**Major Argumentative Moves/Upshots**

*Question*: Is valence, as employed in neuroscientific explanations, genuinely *representational*?

*Answer*: Valence is genuinely representational because its explanatory role as a content-general signal of *goodness*/*badness* in the production of appetitive/aversive behaviors.
- The content-general signal characterization gains explanatory purchase in the way that valence can *misrepresent* and *flexibly* motivate behaviors over time.

*Argumentative Strategy*: I first (1.) demonstrate that valence is representational on a recent iteration of teleosemantics --- namely, Shea's *varietal semantics*. Second (2.) I argue that the *particular* case of valence is resilient against criticisms that beset this account of representation.

1. Following Varietal Semantics, systems are representational when they operate on exploitable relations with features of the environment *in virtue* of serving a *task function*. That is, the system functions to produce certain outcomes that are both *robust* and *stabilized*.  
   2. **Stable**: Valence, as conceived in the case of fruit flies, operates to stabilize the production of appetitive and aversive behaviors by both contributing to the fitness or survival of the organism and its ability to learn over time.  
      1. Within the valence processing of fruit flies, there are innate *hardwired* processes that produce behaviors contributing to the survival of the organism; e.g., hardwired processes that predispose the organism toward approaching fitness-enhancing properties in its environment.  
      2. Second, through parallel pathways, valence processing is essential to the organism’s ability to learn over time. Memory of valence is encoded through the strength of synaptic connections, allowing the organism to track apt actions in the environment over time.  
   3. **Robust**: The mechanisms implicated in valence processing are robust at many levels of explanation.  
      1. First, there are many sensory mechanisms that feed into the core mechanisms required for valence processing. Furthermore, these sensory mechanisms operate across a variety of environmental conditions.  
      2. Second, the process from sensory information to valence is also quite robust. As indicated above, there are parallel pathways for hardwired versus learned signals.  
      3. Finally, the route from valence to the production of action (appetitive or aversive) arises from the aggregation of many valence signals and other mechanisms required for action.  

4. Ultimately, I argue that valence serves as a primitive directive content in virtue its role as a content-general signal of good/bad.  
   5. Valence is a content-general signal of good/bad.  
      1. It is content-general in the sense that it produces signals that are employed *systematically* in the production of behaviors.  
      2. Put differently, the content of valence cannot be reduced to, say, conjunctions of descriptions of the specific inputs that guide the production of behavior.  
      3. That is, for the same reasons that make valence mechanisms robust also point to the content generality of valence:  
         1. Though many sensory mechanisms serve as inputs to valence mechanisms, valence encoding itself can operate independently of these sensory inputs. Furthermore, there is evidence to suggest that valence processes do not carry detailed information about those sensory mechanisms.  
         2. Lastly, the processes that describe the encoding of positive/negative valence that contribute to immediate behaviors function as general variables, not strict mappings between signals and actions.  
      4. These signals carry directive content of goodness and badness:  
         1. Valence signals play a necessary role in explaining the production of specific behaviors --- namely, appetitive and aversive behaviors.  
            1. That is, organisms such as the fruit fly have behavioral repertoires that tend to pattern as either appetitive or aversive; e.g., flying toward, orienting the body, touching. Explaining why these specific behaviors cluster together helps demonstrate the explanatory role of valence and why it carries distinct signals --- good and bad.  
         2. Lastly, the fact that valence encodes general signals of good/bad helps explain how the system can *misrepresent*:  
            1. As mentioned earlier, valence contributes to the learned behaviors of an organism. In this sense, maladaptive behaviors can be acquired through malfunction—for example, encoding a positive signal for novel stimuli that would otherwise have been hardwired as bad.  
 **Upshots**
- Though the notion of valence as a form of representation may lead some to balk at the idea that it is a genuine representation, understanding these mechanisms at the level of neuroscience is important for a full theory of valence.  
- That is, although valence as employed in this paper is not a distinctively psychological sense of representation, understanding valence at this level (regardless of whether one counts it as representational) is necessary for understanding valence representations in psychology.  
- Lastly, I explore whether valence could be conceptually primitive, in that it does not rely on other antecedent evaluative or directive concepts.  
- I also explore the possibility that this sense of valence could support *desire-first* views of representation.
